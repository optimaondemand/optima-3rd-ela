About the Book

The Whipping Boy won the Newbery Medal. Prince Brat is the spoiled son of a king. He plays pranks all day, but it is forbidden to spank a prince, so a boy from the streets named Jemmy is kept in the castle to be punished in his place. When the bored prince runs away and drags Jemmy along, the two boys end up in a world where being a prince doesn’t count for much.

It is short and funny, with chapter titles like “In which we observe a hair-raising event.” We read it because it gives third graders big questions to argue about: What is fair? Who really has power? Can two very different people learn to trust each other?

How a Week Works

Days 1–3
Read one or two chapters, practice the week’s word study (a word part, a spelling pattern, a grammar skill and new vocabulary), answer three chapter questions and take one step in the opinion writing.
Day 4
A review day: look back over the week’s chapters, review the word study, and check the writing.
Fluency
One short passage from the book, read three times and recorded in Canvas.
Check-In
A short Canvas quiz at the end of the week on that week’s skills and chapters.

Your Student’s Opinion Writing

Writer’s Workshop

Across the unit, your student writes an opinion about a character in the book. It starts with a claim (“Prince Brat is the most unfair character because…”), then adds reasons and examples from the book, gets drafted, revised and published. An example writer named Clara shows each step first.

Good to Know Ahead of Time

Whippings
Jemmy is hit with a switch as punishment for the prince’s pranks. The book tells it with humor and nothing is graphic, but it may lead to questions about fairness and punishment.
The outlaws
Hold-Your-Nose Billy and Cutwater are highwaymen who rob travelers. They call themselves dangerous, take the boys and plan to trade the prince for gold. Jemmy also worries out loud that he could be hanged.
How Jemmy talks
Jemmy grew up on the streets and talks that way (“ain’t,” “me rags”). The lessons explain that the author did this on purpose so readers can hear his voice.
A made-up kingdom
The castle and kingdom have no name and aren’t a real place. The lessons use real old paintings, so the pictures your student sees are from history, not from the book.
🤝

How to Help (Without Doing It for Them)

Try this

  • Ask “Where in the book does it say that?” when your student answers a question.
  • Point them to Opti’s tips and the hint buttons before you step in.
  • Ask the questions in each weekly guide. Talking it through first makes the writing easier.
  • Be the audience for the fluency reading and the finished opinion writing.

Try to avoid

  • Typing or wording answers for them. Teachers need to see your student’s own thinking.
  • Fixing their writing. Revising is a lesson of its own in Week 21.
  • Telling them how the book ends. The surprises are part of the fun.
