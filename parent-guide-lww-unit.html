About the Book

The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe was published in 1950. C.S. Lewis was a professor in England. During World War II, four children, Peter, Susan, Edmund and Lucy, are sent away from London to stay in an old Professor’s house in the country. Lucy steps into a wardrobe and finds Narnia, a land where it is always winter and never Christmas, ruled by the White Witch. The children meet talking animals, learn about a great lion named Aslan, and have to decide whom to trust.

We read it because it is a wonderful first fantasy novel, and because it gives third graders big things to think about: honesty, courage, forgiveness, and what it costs to make a wrong choice and then try to put it right.

How a Week Works

Days 1–2
Read the week’s chapters, practice the week’s word study (a word part, a spelling pattern, a grammar skill and new vocabulary), answer three chapter questions and take one writing step.
Days 3–4
Look back at the same chapters for exact words and evidence, keep practicing the word study, and finish the week’s writing step.
Fluency
One short passage from the book, read three times and recorded in Canvas.
Check-In
A short Canvas quiz at the end of the week on that week’s skills and chapters.

Your Student’s Literary Response Writing

Writer’s Workshop

Across the unit, your student writes literary responses: short pieces that say what they think about the book and prove it with details and short quotes from the book. They start by describing the setting of Narnia, then explain a character’s choice, compare two characters, write about a symbol or theme, and finish with a final revision in Week 30. An example writer named Clara shows each step first.

Good to Know Ahead of Time

A real war
The story begins in World War II. The children were sent away from London because of air raids, when planes dropped bombs on the city. The book mentions this only briefly.
The White Witch
The Witch is truly frightening. She turns creatures into stone statues, and in Chapter 2 Mr. Tumnus admits he was supposed to hand Lucy over to her. Later, she has Mr. Tumnus taken away.
Aslan’s death
In Chapters 13–15, Aslan gives his life in Edmund’s place and is killed by the Witch and her creatures on the Stone Table. It is a sad, serious scene. He comes back to life the next morning.
A battle
In Chapter 12, Peter fights a wolf with a sword. Near the end there is a battle, and Edmund is badly wounded and then healed. Nothing is described in gory detail.
Edmund’s choices
Edmund lies, is unkind to Lucy, and goes to the Witch. The lessons treat this as a choice with costs, and the book shows him being forgiven and changing.
A Christian author
Lewis was a Christian, and many readers see the story of Aslan as a picture of the Christian story. The lessons teach it as a novel and focus on the characters, choices and writing.

How to Help (Without Doing It for Them)

Try this

  • Ask “Where in the book does it say that?” when your student answers a question.
  • Point them to Opti’s videos and the hint buttons before you step in.
  • Ask the questions in each weekly guide. Talking it through first makes the writing easier.
  • Be the audience for the fluency reading and the finished literary response.

Try to avoid

  • Typing or wording answers for them. Teachers need to see your student’s own thinking.
  • Fixing their writing. Revising is a lesson of its own in Week 30.
  • Telling them what happens next. The surprises are part of the fun.
