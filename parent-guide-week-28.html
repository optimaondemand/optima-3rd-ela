This Week at a Glance

In Chapter 10, the Beavers and the children escape in the night. In the morning they hear sleigh bells, but it is not the Witch: it is Father Christmas, who gives each of them a gift and says the Witch’s magic is weakening. Chapter 11 follows Edmund on the Witch’s sledge, until the snow starts to melt so fast that the sledge gets stuck and spring comes to Narnia. In Chapter 12, the others reach the Stone Table and meet Aslan, and Peter fights his first battle. Day 1 reads Chapter 10, Day 2 Chapter 11, Day 3 Chapter 12, and Day 4 looks back at all three.

Reading skill
Symbols and repeated ideas. A symbol is a thing in a story that stands for a bigger idea. Good readers notice what keeps coming back and ask what it stands for.
Big idea
What does it mean when something in a story stands for more than just itself? The snow, the thaw and Father Christmas all point to something bigger.

“Aslan is on the move. The Witch’s magic is weakening.”Father Christmas, Chapter 10

Words to Know

Use them at home!
renewedmade new again; restored to a fresh state
heralda person or thing that announces something important is coming
solemnserious, formal, and sincere
jovialcheerful and friendly; in high spirits
Word parts
The word parts com- and con- both mean with or together.combine · connect · company · conversation
Spelling
Some endings change the base word first: double the last letter, drop the silent e, or change y to i.run → running · hope → hoping · carry → carried
Grammar
An apostrophe has two jobs. It joins two words, or it shows who something belongs to.did not → didn’t · the bottle that belongs to Lucy → Lucy’s bottle

New in the Lessons

🌼 The Great Thaw
This week’s word game is a snowy glade in Narnia. On Days 1 and 2 your student picks what a com- or con- word means. On Days 3 and 4 they decide which change a base word needs before an ending goes on (double, drop the e, change y to i, or none). Each right answer melts a snow patch and a spring flower from Chapter 11 comes up, and by Day 4 the whole glade has thawed.

🌍 World Notes
Each day opens the Reading tab with a real thing behind the story: the old English Father Christmas, the real spring flowers Edmund sees (snowdrops, celandines, crocuses), how knights were made long ago, and real wolves, which are shy of people and not like the villains in old stories.
🏮 Truth Watch
After reading, your student decides if three moments show someone being honest: Yes or Not yet. This week includes the Witch’s broken promise to Edmund, a little squirrel who tells the truth, and Peter owning up to his part in Edmund going wrong.

Each day opens with real art, never pictures from the book: Father Christmas from a London newspaper at Christmas in 1848, Isaac Levitan’s painting of melting snow in March (1895), Edmund Blair Leighton’s The Accolade, a painting of a knight being made (1901), and Vincent van Gogh’s Almond Blossom (1890), which he painted for a newborn baby as a sign of new life. Your student looks first, then taps to learn about it.

Good to Know

Chapter 11 is hard for Edmund: the Witch is cruel to him, slaps him, ties his hands, and turns a happy party of animals into stone. In Chapter 12, a huge wolf named Maugrim chases Susan up a tree, and Peter fights it with his sword and kills it. The fight is short, but it is the scariest part of the book so far. Peter is not hurt, and afterward Aslan makes him a knight. If your student is worried, it helps to talk about how Peter was scared and did the brave thing anyway.

Ask at Home

  1. Why was it such big news when Father Christmas got into Narnia?Listen for: the Witch made it always winter and never Christmas; her magic is weakening.
  2. What does the melting snow stand for?Listen for: the Witch losing her power, hope, Aslan coming; any answer with a reason from the book.
  3. Was Peter brave in Chapter 12, even though he felt sick with fear?Listen for: yes, because he did what he had to do anyway.
  4. Where does the apostrophe go in “didnt”? And in “Lucys bottle”?Listen for: didn’t (where the o was), Lucy’s (the bottle belongs to Lucy).
